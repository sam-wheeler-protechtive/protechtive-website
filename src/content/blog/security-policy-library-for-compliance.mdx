When companies first start building a security program, policies are often the last thing they want to think about. They feel bureaucratic — a checkbox exercise that produces documents nobody reads. Then an auditor asks for them, a prospect's security questionnaire demands them, or cyber insurance renewal requires evidence they exist. Suddenly the policy library that was supposed to happen eventually becomes urgent.

The problem isn't that most companies lack policies. It's that most companies have *bad* policies: downloaded templates from the internet, never customized, never reviewed, never enforced. Those don't satisfy a SOC 2 auditor. They don't meet HIPAA requirements. And they don't provide any actual security value.

Here's how to build a policy library that works.

## What Auditors Are Actually Looking For

Policies serve two distinct purposes: they communicate expectations to employees, and they provide auditors with documented evidence of your control environment. A policy that does neither is worse than useless — it creates liability by documenting a standard you're not meeting.

When auditors review policies, they check three things:

1. **Completeness** — does the policy address the required topics for the framework under review?
2. **Accuracy** — does the policy reflect what you actually do? A policy claiming monthly access reviews that happen quarterly is a finding.
3. **Currency** — has the policy been reviewed within the required timeframe? SOC 2 expects annual review cycles.

Generic templates fail on accuracy, almost by definition. A policy template that describes a generic "patch management process" isn't describing your patch management process. When the auditor asks for evidence that the process in the policy is being followed, you have a problem.

## The Core Policy Set

Every mid-market company pursuing SOC 2, HIPAA, or NIST CSF compliance needs a set of foundational policies. This isn't an exhaustive list, but it's the core:

**Information Security Policy** — The master document establishing your security program's scope, objectives, and governance structure. References your other policies. Required by virtually every framework.

**Acceptable Use Policy** — What employees can and can't do with company systems, data, and networks. One of the first policies auditors request and one most companies already have — usually in outdated form.

**Access Control Policy** — How access is provisioned, reviewed, and revoked. Must match your actual processes: if you do quarterly access reviews, the policy says quarterly.

**Data Classification and Handling Policy** — How you categorize sensitive data and what protections apply to each category. Required for HIPAA (defines PHI handling) and foundational for SOC 2 confidentiality criteria.

**Incident Response Policy** — How you detect, respond to, and recover from security incidents. Must include notification requirements if you handle regulated data.

**Change Management Policy** — How changes to systems and infrastructure are reviewed, approved, and deployed. SOC 2 change management controls depend on this existing and being followed.

**Vulnerability Management Policy** — How vulnerabilities are identified, prioritized, and remediated. Should define your SLAs (critical vulnerabilities remediated within X days) and reflect what you actually achieve.

**Business Continuity and Disaster Recovery Policy** — Your RPO/RTO commitments, backup requirements, and recovery testing cadence.

**Third-Party Risk Management Policy** — How you assess and monitor vendors with access to your systems or data. Required for SOC 2 vendor management controls and HIPAA Business Associate management.

## The Customization Problem

The reason template policies fail audits is that they describe a generic company, not your company. Every policy that references a process needs to describe *your* process.

Before writing any policy, document how the control actually operates today — even if the answer is "inconsistently." Then write the policy to reflect a realistic, achievable standard. If your current state doesn't match the required standard, the policy becomes a gap analysis: here's where we are, here's the process we're building toward, here's the timeline.

This honesty matters. An auditor who finds a gap between your stated policy and your actual operation is noting a finding. An auditor who sees a policy with a documented improvement plan is evaluating your risk management maturity.

## Review Cycles and Ownership

Policies don't maintain themselves. Assign an owner to each policy — the person accountable for its accuracy and for driving the annual review. For most mid-market companies, this means distributing ownership: HR owns the Acceptable Use Policy, IT owns Access Control and Patch Management, Legal or the CISO owns the master Information Security Policy.

Set calendar reminders. Build the annual review into your SOC 2 or compliance calendar. When the review happens, document it: who reviewed, what changed, who approved. That documentation is what auditors want to see.

## How Policies Connect to Your Compliance Program

Policies aren't compliance by themselves — they're the documented foundation your controls sit on. Every SOC 2 control points back to a policy that defines the requirement. Every HIPAA control maps to a policy that specifies how you meet the rule. NIST CSF treats policy documentation as a core element of program maturity.

Organizations that build accurate, maintained policy libraries find compliance audits significantly smoother. The evidence collection is easier because the controls are clearly defined. The gap identification is faster because there's a documented standard to measure against.

More importantly, a good policy library communicates professionalism to enterprise prospects. When a security questionnaire asks "do you have a documented information security policy?" the answer shouldn't be "we think so."
